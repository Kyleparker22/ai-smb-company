The four questions you’re about to ask.

You weren’t on the call, so here are the answers without the call.

What is this, exactly?

A set of AI agents that do specific jobs inside the business — answering the phone, chasing a quote, prepping an invoice — coordinated so work passes between them instead of landing back on a person. We build it and we run it. Nobody at your end manages agents, picks models, or babysits a dashboard.

The comparison people reach for is software. It’s closer to hiring: you’re buying the work getting done, not a tool that helps someone do it.

What does it replace?

Mostly it replaces work that isn’t currently getting done — the follow-up nobody had time for, the call that went to voicemail at 6pm, the quote that sat for three days. That’s where the money usually is, and it’s why this rarely turns into a headcount conversation.

Where it does touch existing work, it takes the repetitive half and leaves the judgment. Anything that reaches a customer or moves money waits for a human to approve it. That’s a design decision, not a setting.

What happens in the first 48 hours?

One capability goes live — the highlighted module above, usually. Not the whole system: the single piece that stops the most expensive leak, running for real, with a human approving anything that leaves the building.

Everything else on the board is the growth path. Which parts, and in what order, is what the audit decides.

What’s the risk if it goes wrong?

Bounded on purpose. The agents cannot send email, delete anything, or run commands — those are denied at the configuration layer, not left to policy. Every action is logged with what it did, why, and what it cost. If something drifts, it’s our problem to catch, and we publish which of our own controls have actually been attacked and which haven’t.
