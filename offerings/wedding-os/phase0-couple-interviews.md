# Phase 0 — Couple interview guide

> Field instrument for the 8–10 couple interviews in [`SPEC.md`](SPEC.md) §10. Feeds decisions **D1**
> (which buyer first) and **D4** (margin), and gates [`product-couples.md`](product-couples.md).
>
> **Run 8–10.** Roughly 35 minutes. Third and last of the Phase 0 guides — the D1 synthesis across all
> three is at the bottom.

---

## What's different about this interview

The other two guides interview a *business about a current problem*. This one interviews **two people
about something that already happened** — and that changes the instrument completely.

| | Planner / Venue | Couple |
|---|---|---|
| Tense | What hurts now | What hurt then |
| Main risk | Politeness, flattering self-report | **Memory reconstruction** |
| The arithmetic | ROI against their gross | None — no P&L exists |
| Willingness to pay | Business case | Emotional, budget-relative, **decided before the pain** |

---

## ⚠️ The two things that make this interview hard

### 1. Happy-ending bias

The day went well, so the year gets remembered fondly. Couples systematically under-report the stress
of planning once they're on the other side of a good wedding. Ask *"was planning stressful?"* six
months out and you'll get *"honestly it was fine, it all came together."*

**The antidote is artifacts, not questions.** Ask them to open the actual spreadsheet, the email
folder, the group chat. The evidence hasn't forgotten. *"You've got 41 emails in that thread"* beats
any recollection.

### 2. ⚠️ The timing mismatch — the sharpest problem with product 1

**A couple would buy this at the start of planning. They don't feel the pain until month three.**

At engagement they're euphoric and confident — *"how hard can it be, people do this all the time."*
That's the moment the purchase decision gets made, and it's the moment they're least willing to pay.
By the time they're drowning, they've already decided not to spend on help and are sunk-cost committed
to doing it themselves.

If that's confirmed, **product 1 cannot be sold directly at the moment of need** — which is a strong
argument for the venue channel, where the OS is handed to them at booking: date just locked, planning
about to start, pain not yet felt, and the credibility borrowed from a venue they already trust.

**Questions 4 and 11 test this directly. It may be the most important thing these interviews produce.**

## Before you start

**Who.** 8–10 couples married in the **last 3–12 months**. Too recent and they're still glowing; older
than a year and it's all reconstruction. Warm network first — this is the easiest of the three groups
to recruit, and the one where a friend-of-a-friend intro costs nothing.

**Get a spread.** At least two who used a planner, at least four who didn't, and if you can find one, a
couple whose wedding had something go wrong. Budget range matters too — a $12k wedding and a $60k
wedding are different products.

**Talk to the driver, ideally with the other one there.** One person usually ran it. Interview them —
but the partner's answer to *"what did they spend all that time on?"* is a useful cross-check on the
happy-ending bias, and often funnier and more honest.

**Be gracious.** Weddings carry family conflict, money stress and disappointment. If something lands
sore, move on. You're not entitled to the story.

**Notes.** Consented recording (Granola, `processes/meeting-capture.md`). Quotes are the deliverable.

---

## The rules

The four from the planner guide, plus one that only applies here:

1. **Ask about their past, never their future.**
2. **Ask them to open things.** Their inbox, their spreadsheet, their notes app. This guide's version
   of the venue "show me the timestamps" move — for memory, not for flattery.
3. **Never mention the product until the interview is over.**
4. **Shut up.**
5. **Don't correct their memory.** If the artifacts say 41 emails and they said "a few," note both.
   The gap *is* the data. Making them feel wrong ends the interview's usefulness.

## The interview

### Warm-up (5 min)

**1. Congratulations — tell me about the wedding. When was it, where, roughly how many people?**
*You need:* date, guest count, and eventually **total budget**. Ask for budget plainly later (Q9), not
here.

**2. Which of you did most of the planning?**
*Establishes the driver.* Everything after is addressed to them.

---

### Part A — What actually happened (12 min)

**3. Walk me through the first month after you got engaged. What did you actually do?**
*Open, unsteered.* Listen for the order they did things in, and whether they knew what the order should
be.

**4. When did you first think "this is more than I expected"?**

> ### ⚠️ Question 4 is the timing test.
> Get a *month*, not a feeling. Month one? Month four? After booking the venue?
> Then follow up: **"At that point, had you already decided whether to hire help?"**
>
> If the pain reliably arrives *after* the help decision, product 1's direct-sale moment doesn't exist
> and the venue channel isn't just cheaper CAC — it's the only viable route.

**5. Take the photographer, or the florist — whichever you remember better. How many did you contact,
and how many got back to you?**
*The wedge test, same as the planner guide.* Get the two numbers.
*Follow-up:* **Can you open that email folder?** How many did you have to chase? Did any just never
reply? What did you do about it?
*⚠️ This is where the artifact matters most — the real count is usually far worse than the memory.*

**6. When you had a few quotes side by side, how did you decide?**
*Follow-up:* Could you actually compare them? Was anything included in one and not another that you
only noticed later? **Did any cost surprise you on the day or after?**
*Listen for:* overtime, vendor meals, cake-cutting fees, travel — the traps the contract reader flags.

**7. What fell through the cracks? What did you find out too late?**
*Follow-up:* Was there a deadline you nearly missed, or did miss?

**8. Was there a point where you thought about giving up and just hiring someone?**
*Listen for:* what stopped them — price, or something else.

---

### Part B — Money and delegation (10 min)

**9. Roughly what did the wedding cost in total? And did you hire a planner or a day-of coordinator?**

*If they hired a planner:* What did they cost, and what did they actually do for you? Was it worth it?
What did you still end up doing yourself?
*If they didn't:* What made you decide not to? Did you look into it and see prices?

> **This is the willingness-to-pay evidence**, and it's behavioural rather than hypothetical. A couple
> who looked at a $6,000 planner and said no has told you something real about a $3,000 alternative.

**10. Did you pay for anything else to help — an app, a paid tier, a template, a course?**
*Listen for:* The Knot/Zola paid features, a Notion template, a spreadsheet someone sold them.
*Follow-up:* What did you use The Knot or Zola for, and where did it stop being useful?
*This tests the aligned-incentives wedge* — did they ever notice those platforms are vendor-funded?

**11. Imagine you're back at week one, before any of it started. Someone offers to do the vendor
research, send all the inquiries, chase everyone who doesn't reply, and put the quotes side by side —
for [$2,500–3,500]. Yes or no?**

> The one hypothetical in the guide, and it's deliberately placed **after** they've relived the whole
> thing so the answer is informed rather than idle. Then the follow-ups that actually matter:
> - **"What would your answer have been in week one, before you knew?"** ← the timing mismatch again
> - "What would have made it an easy yes?"
> - "What would have made you not trust it?"

**12. Who else was helping you — family, bridesmaids? What did you hand off, and what could you not
bring yourself to hand off?**
*Behavioural delegation evidence.* What a person actually delegated to a trusted human is a better
predictor than what they say they'd delegate to software.

---

### Part C — The trust question (5 min)

**13. Would you have been comfortable with software emailing vendors on your behalf, in your name?**

> ### ⚠️ Question 13 decides whether this product exists.
> *Follow-ups, and these are where the real answer lives:*
> - What specifically would have worried you?
> - Would it change if you approved every message before it sent?
> - What about *after* you'd booked someone — the same, or different?
>
> Expect the answer to split: comfortable for initial outreach to strangers, much less comfortable once
> a vendor is booked. **That's exactly the R1 boundary in `SPEC.md` §5**, and hearing couples draw it
> themselves would be strong confirmation the autonomy design is right.

**14. Anything you'd tell someone who just got engaged?**
*A gift of a closing question — they'll enjoy answering it, and the answer is often a clean statement
of the product's value proposition in their own words.*

**15. Who else should I talk to?**

## The transition

Same rule. Interview first, then:

> *"That's everything I wanted to ask. Can I tell you what I'm working on?"*

With couples there's no deal at the end — they're already married. **What you want here is the referral
in Q15 and, if they're enthusiastic, permission to quote them.** Ask for that explicitly.

## Kill criteria — pre-committed

| If… | Then |
|---|---|
| **Most say week-one no to Q11 even after reliving it** | Direct sale doesn't work. Product 1 is a **channel product only** — sold through venues and planners, never marketed to couples. |
| **Q13 comes back mostly no** | The product as scoped is dead. Re-scope to draft-only, where the couple sends everything — which is a much weaker product and a much harder sale. |
| **Most used a planner already** | The underserved-DIY segment is smaller than assumed. It also means the planner product's market is the bigger one. |
| **Willingness to pay clusters under ~$1,000** | The unit economics collapse against operator approval time (D4). Re-scope or don't build. |
| **Q5's real numbers show vendors mostly replied fine** | The wedge is weaker than assumed for *everyone*, not just planners. That would undermine all three products — escalate, don't proceed. |
| **"Honestly it was fine"** — even with artifacts open | The pain is overstated. Believe the artifacts over the sentiment, but if both say fine, that's the answer. |

## The D1 synthesis — after all three guides are run

Phase 0 exists to answer **D1: which buyer first.** Lay the three sets side by side:

| | Planners | Venues | Couples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is the chasing wedge real *for them*? | Q3 | n/a | Q5 |
| Is there a measurable revenue lever? | Q6/Q7 capacity | **Q4 timestamps** | none |
| Can they pay, and does the math clear? | % of gross | ROI per booking | Q11 |
| How long is the sale? | One owner | Q14 authority | Impulse, but mistimed |
| Can they be reached affordably? | Warm/IG | Direct | **Only via the other two** |

**Then ask the two questions that actually decide it:**

1. **Where is the pain sharpest?** If couples have the pain and planners don't (the kill finding in the
   planner guide), then the pain and the money sit with different buyers — and the answer is the
   channel play, not a buyer choice.
2. **What can yourco afford?** At $0 cash, a six-month venue cycle may be correct and unaffordable at
   the same time. The fastest yes may beat the best customer.

**Write the D1 answer down as a decision** (`log-decision`), with a `**Locks:** Wedding OS buyer` line
so it's a real call and not a lingering preference. If the answer is "none of them yet," that is a
legitimate and valuable outcome — it cost fifteen conversations instead of a build.

---

## The one-sentence reminder

You're not asking whether planning a wedding is stressful — everyone says yes. **You're finding out
when the pain arrived, whether it arrived before or after they decided about help, and whether they'd
have let software send an email in their name.**
