# Lead high, land wherever they'll start — the expansion motion is the upside, not the discount

## Decision

The offering hierarchy is **unchanged**: custom AI OS → a few coordinated modules → a single digital employee. Every proposal still **opens with the OS** shaped from the Audit.

What changes is what a small start *means*. **Landing below the OS is a good outcome, not a failed one** — the expansion path is planned from day one rather than improvised later, and account growth, not discounting, is where the revenue is expected to come from. the Founder's framing: *"we will have more success upselling rather than downselling."*

**Locks:** nothing — this refines the sales stance inside the existing hierarchy and does not lock a walkthrough domain.

## Context

The hierarchy has been settled since 2026-06-16 and narrowed on 2026-06-18: the unit of sale is the OS or its first module, the digital employee is the smallest module and the entry rung, and the employee-shopping surfaces are demos rather than a menu.

Two things in that framing pulled against each other, and it surfaced on 2026-08-10 the night before the plan lock. The written motion is a **down-sell**: lead with the OS, offer the single employee last. But the belief the Founder actually holds is that **upsell will outperform** — that it is easier to grow an account already inside the moat than to win a bigger one by cutting price. Read literally, "offered last" makes the smallest unit a concession you retreat to, which is exactly the framing that makes an upsell awkward later: you have already told the buyer this was the lesser option.

Nothing about the ranking was wrong. The word doing the damage was "down-sell."

## Options considered

- **Keep it exactly as written.** Lead high, single employee last, no change. Rejected as insufficient — it leaves "down-sell" describing the motion we expect most first deals to actually take, and pre-frames the on-ramp as a loss.
- **Flip the motion — land small deliberately, then upsell.** Open with a single employee as the entry product and expand into the OS. Rejected: it reverses 2026-06-16 and 2026-06-18, re-creates the menu-SKU problem the narrowing removed, anchors price low before the Audit has shown the buyer what is broken, and would ripple into the site, packets, pricing bands, audit SOP and proposal template for a thesis with zero closed deals behind it.
- **Chosen: lead high, land anywhere, plan the expansion from day one.** The opener does not change; the interpretation of a small close does.

## Why

The Audit is what makes leading high credible — it produces the evidence for the larger scope, so opening with the OS is opening with the diagnosis, not with the biggest number. Abandoning that to lead with a single employee would throw away the one asset that justifies the price.

But the expected first deal is small, and the plan should say so without calling it a defeat. A first module that is genuinely the first *module* — scoped as part of a system the client has already seen mapped — is a different object from a standalone employee sold as the cheap option. The first is an on-ramp with a road attached; the second is a product line we deliberately do not have.

This also matches where the margin is. Expansion inside an account costs no CAC, arrives with delivery trust already earned, and lands on infrastructure already built — which is precisely the "account expansion" stage the delivery loop already ends on. Winning bigger logos by discounting does the opposite: it buys revenue with margin and sets the anchor low permanently.

**The honest caveat:** this is a belief, not a finding. Zero audits have been delivered, zero clients signed, and no expansion has ever happened. The one live data point runs the other way — Sample Client sits unsigned at proposal at $1,000/mo against a $3,000 Core floor, which is a small landing that has not yet expanded or closed.

## Reversibility

Cheap to revisit and deliberately so — this changes language and sales posture, not structure, pricing, or any built surface. The hierarchy, the tier ladder and the Audit-first motion are all untouched, so reversing this means editing prose.

What would make it wrong is evidence that small landings *don't* expand: clients who buy one module and stay there. That is measurable the moment there are clients, and the CRM's expansion tracking is where it would show.

## Trip-wire

- **Review:** 2027-02-10
- **Overturn if:** three or more clients have landed below the OS and none has expanded within six months — that would mean the on-ramp is a terminus and leading high is costing us deals we then fail to grow. Also overturn if the reverse appears: clients routinely buying the full OS on the first proposal, which would make the whole down-sell/up-sell question moot and argue for pricing higher.
- **Check:** `signedClients >= 3`
- **Check covers:** only the precondition that enough clients exist to judge. Expansion-within-six-months is not instrumented — the CRM records deal stage, not module count over time — so a firing check means "go read the accounts," never "this decision is confirmed."
