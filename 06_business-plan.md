## 1 · Owner's principles

These are the standing rules of the company. They change rarely, and never quietly.

1. **We are an operator, not a vendor.** YourCo designs, builds, and *runs* custom AI operating systems inside other companies. The client buys an outcome and never touches a token, a model, a prompt, or a server. An engagement that would leave the client holding the machinery, we decline.

2. **Diagnosis precedes prescription.** Every engagement begins with an Audit: find the bottleneck, put a dollar figure on it, build against that. The Audit exists because the bottleneck differs per business.

3. **We sell the system, never the menu.** The unit of sale is the custom AI OS or its first module. A single named digital employee — live in the client's tenant in roughly 48 hours — is the smallest on-ramp, offered last, never a SKU.

4. **Our margin lives in the layer nobody else wants to build.** Reliability, evaluation, observability, approval flow, integration, audit trail. Anyone with twenty dollars a month can wire a workflow; almost nobody can prove the workflow did its job in front of the person whose name is on the building. We charge for the proof.

5. **We absorb the model spend.** Token, voice, telephony, and hosting costs are ours, invisible to the client. A high model bill alongside landing outcomes is good news — expensive human labor got replaced on both sides of the invoice. This is the business model, not a leak in it.

6. **Model progress is our dividend.** We own the stack and sell the outcome, so every AI improvement is swapped in beneath the client at no charge. What we sell appreciates as AI advances; what our clients could buy instead depreciates. This is the standing answer to the one objection every buyer has.

7. **Three things we will not do.** No self-serve (it hands the eval risk back to the customer and deletes the reason we exist). No fabricated proof (no invented metrics or testimonials; pre-revenue outcomes are described qualitatively, and that is stated, not disguised). No prices on the public site (prices live in proposals, where scope is real).

8. **Autonomy is earned, never granted.** Every agent starts behind an approval gate and earns each action toward full autonomy on eval evidence. Day-one autonomy on high-stakes actions is how this category will destroy itself; we intend to be the counterexample.

9. **We hire humans slowly and deliberately.** Automate first; every hire is justified by client load the AI plus the principals can no longer absorb. We measure revenue per human and expect the figure to embarrass a normal agency.

10. **Every number arrives with its assumption attached.** Where a figure rests on something unvalidated, we say so in the same sentence. The most sensitive are named in Section 8, before the projections they feed.

11. **Bad news travels first.** In this document and in how we operate, the unflattering fact leads. A plan that hides its weakest link from its own owners will eventually hide it from clients.

12. **We keep score on four numbers.** Recurring retainer base, logo retention, gross margin after absorbed model costs, revenue per human. Vanity metrics inform tactics and never appear in how we judge ourselves.

13. **Clients, referrals, brand, and reputation are everything.** We are a trust business before we are a technology business. Every delivered outcome is the marketing; every referral is the growth engine; the brand is the promise kept again and again. One careless external act destroys more value than any campaign can buy back — which is why the credibility gate, the white-label rule, and approval-gated sends sit in this list, not in a process doc. When a decision trades reputation for speed or short-term revenue, reputation wins, every time.

14. **Our team and our culture are what scale the business.** The team is two-natured: a small number of humans, hired slowly as the standard-setters (Principle 9), and an agent workforce that carries the culture in code — bad news first, evidence before claims, every loop closed, gates over speed. Culture here is not a poster; it is written into the operating system every agent reads before it acts. People set the bar; the system multiplies it.

15. **We will adapt.** The technology under this company is changing faster than any technology ever has, and nothing in this plan entitles us to a smooth ride. Our answer is structural: the destination holds, the route flexes. We own the stack so model churn arrives as a dividend (Principle 6); we attach assumptions so we know which belief broke when one does (Principle 10); when a blocker stops moving, we re-plan rather than re-wait. A plan is a hypothesis — adapting it on evidence is how the course gets stayed.

16. **Work hard, live free.** A company where people work hard, make the money they want to make, and keep the freedom to live their lives — internally: *get your shit done and reward yourself.* The AI OS absorbs the grind that turns growing companies into burnout machines, so humans are measured on outcomes delivered, never on hours visible. The standards are what make the freedom affordable.

17. **Partners may build other things — after the conversation, never after the fact.** An equity Partner may hold another job or start another business or venture, subject to two conditions and no others. **First, it must not compete with yourco** — not for the same buyer, the same offering, or the same operated-AI lane; where the line is genuinely unclear, that is itself a reason to have the conversation rather than an excuse to skip it. **Second, the Partners must have discussed it *before* it starts** — before the job is accepted, before the entity is formed, before work begins. The timing is the rule. A disclosure after the fact is not a conversation; it is a notification, and it puts the other Partners in the position of objecting to something already underway.

    This is deliberately generous, and it is generous because the alternative is worse: Partners with outside interests either hide them or resent the restriction, and both corrode a three-person company faster than any competitor. the Founder already operates multiple ventures; a rule that pretended otherwise would be fiction on page one.

    **Two things it does not do.** It does not lower the service standard — the Operating Agreement's commitment to devote substantially full working time to yourco stands, and "I may start other ventures" is not "I owe yourco less." Outside work fits around the commitment, not through it. And it is not a waiver: it permits *named, discussed* activity, not outside activity in general. The mechanism in the Operating Agreement is **Schedule C-2**, the list of carved-out ventures — which this rule turns from a fixed schedule into a living one, added to by agreement before each new venture begins.

## 2 · The business we are in

Small and mid-sized businesses lose money to bottlenecks they can name but cannot staff their way out of: missed calls, slow quotes, unworked pipeline, drowning admin, knowledge locked in the owner's head. AI models are now reliable enough to absorb much of that work — but only wrapped in an evaluation and approval layer the business cannot build itself. No-code tools hand the owner a loaded workflow and all the reliability risk. We take the opposite position: we keep the risk, the infrastructure, and the maintenance; the client keeps the outcome.

**The motion is fixed.** A **free Audit** (free while yourco is getting started — 2026-08-16, `decisions/2026-08-16_audit-is-free.md`) diagnoses the business and quantifies its bottlenecks against the eight-pillar taxonomy — Intake/Front Desk · Sales · Marketing · Customer/Retention · Operations/Delivery · Back Office/Finance · Company Brain · People/Training (`processes/ai-os-modules.md`). Then we build and operate a **custom AI OS** — the product and primary revenue generator. Offering hierarchy, high to low: custom AI OS → a few coordinated employees → a single employee (the entry rung, offered last — a good place to land, never the opener). Under every pillar sits the cross-cutting layer never sold as a line item — reliability, eval, observability, approval, audit log — and land-and-expand runs along the same map: land one module, grow the pillar, grow the OS.

**Three form factors**, chosen by what the work needs: **digital employees** (a named agent in the client's tenant, first one live in ~48 hours), **headless automations** (trigger-fired workflows), and **embedded AI platforms** (customer-facing products under the client's brand — an instant-quote design studio for a hardscaper, a storm-verification service for a roofing operator). "No agent attached" is a shape, not a gap — a platform has the AI in the engine rather than wearing a name — and the moat binds per form factor: for a quote surface, eval means a tracked record of estimates against signed prices, with human approval on any firm number. We run all three on ourselves, so every client architecture mirrors one already proven in-house. (Honest qualifier: the 48-hour claim is from our internal build record; validating it on engagement #1 is a Year-0 milestone, not an entitlement.)

**Structure and ownership (2026-08-05).** YourCo LLC, Florida, formed April 2026 (state-record name mismatch being resolved). Currently single-member: the Founder. A **three-member admission at 50 / 35 / 15 is in active draft** (the Founder · Partner B · Partner C, set 2026-08-10, superseding the earlier 50/50 plan), each side held through its own single-purpose holding company — founder side: Holdco LLC (to be formed). The structural decisions hold: each partner's stake is a **profits interest** (a share of everything built from their grant forward; pre-grant value protected by a distribution threshold), now two grants with two vesting clocks. **the Founder at 50% does not control the company** — the two partners together also hold exactly half. Three items a partner's counsel will look for, all open: the voting threshold, the deadlock end-game, and each partner's operating lane and capacity contribution. The company also holds an interest in a live, venture-scale, currently **unpapered** collaboration (gate #11) — counted in no projection until papered.

**Deliberately parked: self-serve SaaS.** Going self-serve deletes the moat — a self-serve product cannot own reliability and approval for the customer. Revisit only if the moat layer becomes so productized that "serve yourself" no longer means "absorb the eval risk yourself."

**The current constraint, without varnish.** A legal matter (OtherVenture) gates external launch. Everything is built and staged for a switch-flip — but the gate's definition, resolution condition, and owner fields are still unfilled, no counsel is engaged, and it is months past its own estimate. A blocker without a definition is a habit, so the plan carries a decision trigger: **if the gate is not defined-and-dated by [the Founder sets date], we re-plan (scope-limited or in-person-first launch per gate #12) rather than re-wait.** The single highest-leverage act available now: engage counsel once, across gates #14 (partner OA), #11 (the collaboration paper), #2 (privacy), and the OtherVenture scope question.

## 3 · The moat, in mechanism terms

**Tooling is commoditizing, and that is the premise, not the threat.** Agent frameworks ship weekly and are a moat for no one, including us — tooling is an input that gets cheaper on someone else's R&D budget. The durable business sits one layer up, where three things must be true at once: the agent's work can be **proven** correct, it is **integrated** into the systems where the business actually lives, and the person accountable for the P&L **trusts** it enough to leave it running. Proof, integration, trust — each slow to build, none purchasable, together exactly what a no-code operator cannot deliver. Every prospect faces three alternatives: a no-code operator or freelancer, a point SaaS tool, or doing nothing. The first two share a defect discovered after purchase — when the workflow breaks in production, the risk belongs to the buyer. Doing nothing remains our largest competitor, which is why the Audit leads with a dollar figure, not a technology description.

In delivery terms, the moat is five artifacts under every engagement: **eval gates** (measurable success criteria), **watchdogs** (runtime guards for drift, errors, cost, scope), **approval flow** (human-in-the-loop where stakes are high, audit trail everywhere), **integration depth**, and **executive-readable reporting**. None is sold as a line item; together they are why the engagement commands a retainer instead of a build fee.

**The model-upgrade dividend** inverts the depreciation logic of buying technology. A tool decays as AI advances; an YourCo outcome improves — better models are swapped in underneath, the outcome gets better, the invoice doesn't change. To "won't this be obsolete in a year?", the truthful answer is that component obsolescence is our maintenance plan, and it runs in the client's favor.

**Autonomy is where the layer earns its keep commercially.** Every action's default trajectory is full autonomy, earned per action on eval evidence, with the client's routine attention trending to zero — the control migrates off the human onto the reliability layer (eval gate, guardrails, watchdog, rollback, kill switch). Done that way, full autonomy is the premium tier; done the cheap way (day-one autonomy, no evidence), it is how a vendor destroys the category. The climb happens on our own operation first, so proof exists before a client is asked to trust it.

**Honest qualifier:** the moat is a design, not yet a demonstrated fact. The machinery is built and runs daily on our own operation — real evidence of a real kind — but it is unproven across a portfolio of paying clients. Until then, "moat" is the name of our hypothesis about where the margin lives, held with conviction and stated as a hypothesis.

## 4 · The offering and how it is priced

**The unit of sale is the OS, or its first module.** Single module/employee: ~$1–5k setup + $1,500–2,500/mo. Three or more coordinated modules = an OS: ~$2–5k implementation + $3–10k/mo, across four levels scoped by the Audit — **Core** (~3 agents) · **Suite** (~5) · **Operation** (~7) · **Command** (up to 10; beyond ten, case-by-case discounted overage). Agent counts are a scoping guide, not a per-agent meter. Polo prices the bands. Embedded platforms price per scope, closer to product builds (worked internal example: an instant-quote platform at ~$5k implementation + ~$500/mo).

**Per-module anchors (the locked OS ladder, `pricing/v0/os-tiers.md`):** **Core** $2–2.5k implementation + **$3–4k/mo** · **Suite** $2.5–3.5k + $4.5–6k/mo · **Operation** $3.5–4.5k + $6.5–8k/mo · **Command** $4.5–5k + $8.5–10k/mo. A single digital employee is the on-ramp ($1–5k setup + $1,500–2,500/mo, capped at 3 before it graduates to an OS). *The tier names are the Founder-locked (2026-06-22); the tier prices remain Polo's v0 draft pending a lock conversation — stated here as the working assumption, not a ratified price sheet.*

**Lead high, land anywhere (2026-08-10).** Every proposal opens with the OS shaped from the Audit — the diagnosis is what makes the larger scope credible, so leading high is leading with evidence rather than with the biggest number. But **a client who starts with one module is a good outcome, not a failed sale.** We expect more of our growth to come from expanding accounts we already run than from discounting to win larger ones: expansion carries no acquisition cost, arrives with delivery trust already earned, and lands on infrastructure already built. The practical consequence is that we stop describing the smallest unit as a *down-sell* — it is the first module of a system the buyer has already seen mapped, and calling it the lesser option is what makes the later expansion conversation harder. ⚠ This is a belief, not a finding: no audit has been delivered, no client has expanded, and the one live deal sits below the Core floor and unsigned.

**Why we price the outcome, not the machinery.** Metering tokens or seats re-exposes the client to the complexity we exist to absorb, and sets our incentive against theirs. A flat retainer with model spend on our side keeps incentives honest — we profit by delivering efficiently, and the bill doesn't flinch when better models swap in. It also makes the dividend real: an upgrade that raised the invoice would not be a dividend.

**Two open pricing items, flagged rather than papered over:** (1) the $1,000 landscaping onboarding price may signal "automation gig" rather than "boutique implementation" — revisit after the first 3–5 deals against close rate and retention; (2) the house rule requires a locked pricing doc per vertical before *cold* outbound, but warm-first targeting means most warm deals land in unlocked verticals — do the horizontal OS bands govern warm/OS deals? Undecided; Polo rules, before the first warm proposal in an unlocked vertical.

**Expansion is the compounding engine.** Land one module, prove it, grow the pillar, grow the OS — each addition a build fee plus retainer step-up, an amendment to a working relationship rather than a new sale. This is why retention (Section 8) is the number that most governs the company's long-run value.

**Unit economics, illustrative and labeled as such:** blended retainer ~$3,100/mo early → ~$4,500 at scale; absorbed cost assumed $300/client/mo (⚠ Sample Product's own ledger models $15–40 — the assumption may be wrong in the favourable direction); gross margin ~86% because AI does the delivery. ## 5 · The market and how we sell

**All industries from day one** — positioning and targeting both horizontal. The sequencing filter is **relationship, not vertical**: warm intros first (they close on trust, prove the model, seed the referral flywheel), then outward. *(Updated 2026-08-05, superseding the landscaping-first beachhead; `decisions/2026-08-05_horizontal-targeting-warm-first.md`.)* The cost of this choice, stated plainly: referral *flow* survives warm-first, but **proof transferability** weakens — "built for a business like mine" closes; "built something for someone" doesn't. So industry depth is **emergent but engineered**: after each close, we deliberately mine that client's industry for the next 2–3 intros.

**The ideal client:** owner-operated SMB, ~1–50 employees, real operational pain AI can absorb — SMB-primary, not exclusive; size never disqualifies a warm intro. The buyer is the owner: single sponsor, no procurement, fast decision. The pain is whatever the Audit finds; the eight pillars are the search space, not a fixed pitch. **Why now:** models are finally reliable enough to operate (with the right eval/approval layer), businesses are AI-curious and can't build it themselves, and the tools they'd reach for leave them holding the risk.

**The GTM engine is built and staged** (nothing ships until the gate clears); playbooks in `processes/`:

- **Warm network first** — the lead motion: relationships across any industry → first clients → first case studies → the referral flywheel.
- **Inbound / product-led** — the Instant Employee as the OS on-ramp ("your first agent") + ROI calculator, capturing to CRM.
- **Proof-led outbound** (when cold resumes) — sourced lists, a *personalized working demo per prospect*, a four-touch sequence; the per-vertical kit points wherever warm traction indicates.
- **Channel partnerships** — agencies, MSPs, vertical SaaS, fractional COOs, bookkeepers on recurring rev-share; the highest-leverage channel for a small team.
- **Content / build-in-public** — the glass-box story of a company that runs itself on AI.
- **Paid ads** — deferred until the funnel converts on its own.

The funnel: proof asset → capture → nurture → call → 48h build → referral → repeat. The 48-hour build is a **trust instrument**, not an engineering boast — it shrinks the distance between "yes" and "working" until the leap of faith is small enough to take. (Which is why it must be validated on a real engagement before being marketed as fact.)

**Current evidence, unflattering as it is:** our warmest prospect — a relationship close, offered near-free founding pricing — has a proposal in hand and has not signed. The first data point on warm close rate currently reads as a caution. Forcing it to a signature or a formal no is a Year-0 milestone; either answer teaches more than silence.

### The flywheel

The growth engine is a loop, and every part of the company exists to spin one stage of it:

**Reach** (warm intros, content, partners put us in front of an owner) → **Trust** (proof-led everything: working demos, the glass-box story, the 48-hour promise shrinking the leap of faith) → **Land** (Audit → first module or OS) → **Outcome** (the system delivers, eval-gated and approval-protected) → **Prove** (the outcome becomes a case study, a testimonial, a number we can say out loud) → **Expand** (the working relationship grows — next module, next pillar, toward the full OS) → **Compound** (the delighted client's referrals feed Reach warmer than we found it; their industry gets mined for the next 2–3 intros; every engagement's learnings make the next delivery faster and better) → back to **Reach**.

Two accelerants spin it faster with no added push: the **model-upgrade dividend** improves every live Outcome for free, which strengthens Prove and Expand without a sales motion; and the **closed-loop OS** turns every engagement into templates and learnings, so each turn of the wheel costs less than the last. And the honest state of the wheel today: **pre-spin.** Every stage is built; none has turned with a paying client. The company currently bootstraps Trust on the one proof it owns outright — yourco runs on its own agents — and the first two or three client outcomes matter more than everything else in this plan combined, because they are what start the wheel.

## 6 · The operating model

YourCo runs on a git-based AI OS — both how the company scales and the standing demo of what it sells. The repository is the brain (SOPs, decisions, learnings), the data (CRM, dashboards, ledgers), and the storefront (the staged site). Work executes when an AI acts in a defined agent role — interactively, or on the **always-on runtime**: 24/7 headless scheduled loops, every one approval-gated. Roughly two dozen named agent roles cover sales ops, copy, content, finance, pricing, compliance, QA, and delivery; the founder conducts. **Always-on does not mean auto-send** — nothing customer-facing leaves without approval, enforced in configuration, not intentions.

The **delivery loop** is the assembly line: discovery converts Audit findings to a build spec → build starts from a golden client template (client logic is an overlay, not a rebuild) → eval gates and watchdogs are written *before* go-live → 48-hour go-live on the first capability → weekly iteration → expansion scoped from inside a working relationship. Internally, every recurring process follows the same closed-loop discipline — a scheduled task, an artifact the next run reads, feedback captured, learnings fed forward — and it is the same mechanism we install in clients.

The leverage in one sentence: most of the company's labor is AI, so revenue can grow far faster than headcount. It is also the honesty check — if the OS cannot run YourCo well, we have no business claiming it will run anyone else's company.

## 7 · People and the partnership

Hire slowly, automate first; every hire is justified by client load the AI plus the principals can no longer absorb.

## 8 · The financial picture

> **`finance/yourco-financial-model.xlsx` is the source of truth for every number in this section.** The workbook holds 6,800+ formulas off one Assumptions sheet; this section carries only headline figures and the reasoning. **When they disagree, the model is right and this section is stale** — that is exactly what happened between 2026-08-05 and 2026-08-10, and the numbers below are the correction.
>
> **The caveats govern the numbers, not the other way around.** Direction, not forecast: nothing here has happened once. Three of the four gaps commissioned to **Polo** are now closed by the model — **(a)** OS-mix is priced off the tier bands × an assumed mix, gliding from band floors to midpoints over 30 months; **(b)** retention sensitivity is built in, with the three cases running 18% / 12% / 8% annual churn; **(d)** the cash line exists and is now fully priced — as of 2026-08-10 the principals are paid from month 1, so no part of the funding requirement hides in unpaid founder time; what remains excluded is legal, CPA, insurance, payment processing and taxes. Still open: **(c)** the Year-1 capacity sanity check — the model now *asserts* three principals carry 50 clients, and nobody has carried one.

**Timeline:** the model runs **Jan-2027 → Dec-2029**. Month 0 is 2026-12-31, projected from 2026-08-05 actuals ($0 cash, $0 MRR, 0 clients); the four months between are assumed to produce no revenue **and no spend**, and the second half of that is not true (~$614/mo of subscriptions).

**Assumptions:** OS tiers per `pricing/v0/os-tiers.md` (Core→Command, $3–10k/mo — ⚠ still an unratified v0 draft); blended retainer **$3,100 early → $4,498 mature**; blended audit fee $1,100; annual churn 18/12/8% by case; COGS $300/client/mo plus an 8% delivery haircut; CAC $1,500; onboarding $150 per new client; equipment $3,000 per person on hire; partner expenses $500 per principal per month; conferences 2/4/6 events a year at $5,000, starting only after the first EBITDA-positive month. ⚠ The "30 principal-hours per new client" figure carried here previously is a **benchmark that is wired into nothing** (`Assumptions!B131`) — it is what the Actuals tab will measure against, not an input the model consumes. Stating it as an assumption made the Year-1 capacity question look tested when it is the one gap of Polo's four still open.

**Read the NOI line with suspicion.** After a Y1 ramp (20–40%, where the fixed principal salaries land on a small revenue base) it **plateaus at 43–50% and stays there** in every case and every remaining year, because nothing in the model creates operating leverage in either direction: commission is a flat 15% of revenue, connector commissions 5%, COGS is per-client, and Advisors are hired in lockstep with client count. A real services business's margin *moves* as it scales. This one can't, by construction — and everything absent (legal, CPA, insurance, payment processing, marketing, equipment, taxes) pushes it down, not up.

**What the principals are paid, and where it sits.** Salary of $50k each **from month 1** — no MRR trigger (the Founder, 2026-08-10; the company pays its principals from the day it starts, funded by the injection below). 5% of revenue each (15% combined) once MRR clears $35k — both in opex, above EBITDA. In the Target case the three-year totals are the Founder **$1.52M**, Partner B **$1.32M**, Mike **$1.05M** — each up ~$21k on the pre-2026-08-10 figures, because the salaries now start in month 1 rather than month 6. ⚠ Every line of that is a Reserved Matter under OA §7.4 and is **not owed to anyone** until the agreement is signed and decision D10 sets the consent threshold.

**The mechanics that matter beneath the table.** Margin: delivery labor is AI at an assumed $300/client/mo against retainers well above it. That assumption is the most consequential unvalidated number in the workbook and it may be badly wrong in the *favourable* direction — Sample Product's own ledger models real run cost at **$15–40/mo**. Retention: at high retention the base compounds; at 65% growth refills a leaking bucket. We have zero retention data. Run-rate vs recognized revenue: run-rate describes the machine at year end, recognized revenue is what the year banked; the durable company is the retainer base.

**Cost structure.** COGS = absorbed model/voice/telephony/hosting. Opex = principal salary + commission, tooling, connector commissions, CAC, onboarding, and Advisors at $10,000/month fully loaded.

**How it is funded (the Founder, 2026-08-10).** the Founder injects **$50,000 on day one**, alone — Partner B and Mike contribute no cash. That plus the pre-formation build spend the Founder has already funded (**~$3,000, the Founder's estimate pending receipt confirmation**) is a **repayable founder loan** of **$53,000**, returned to him as a lump sum in the first month cumulative cash covers it plus a three-month operating reserve — **month 5** in the Target case. The loan is **junior to member distributions**, not senior: distributions are taken first and the Founder is repaid from what remains.

The honest statement is no longer "no case needs more than the injection." It is that **two of three cases fit inside $50,000 with almost nothing to spare, the third needs about $11,000 more, and the second tranche is what makes the plan solvent rather than what makes it comfortable.**

**Equipment, partner expenses and conferences (added 2026-08-10).** Every internal hire gets the equipment they need — laptop, headphones, monitor — at **$3,000 per person**, charged in the month they are hired, three principals in month 1. The principals additionally run **$500 each per month** of legitimate business expense through the company (phone, internet, meals, home office); it is a personal tax-efficiency win and a real cost to the company, and it is charged as one. We plan to attend **conferences and trade shows — 2 in Year 1, 4 in Year 2, 6 in Year 3 at ~$5,000 each** — with spend deliberately starting only *after* the first EBITDA-positive month, so it cannot deepen the trough that decides whether the injection holds. Together these cost about **$28,000 in Year 1** and are the reason the Conservative case now breaches. Still excluded: legal, CPA, insurance, payment processing, taxes.

## 10 · How we could fail

Written now, by us, rather than later as someone else's post-mortem. Ranked by what would actually kill the company; where a mitigation has decayed into a comfort, we say so.

**1. The launch gate never defines itself, and the company polishes until it is stale.** The only failure mode currently in progress. The gate has no written definition, no resolution condition, no engaged counsel, and is months past its estimate. "Keep building internally" was a mitigation; it has aged into a symptom — the building continues because the building is what remains permitted. The mitigation now is structural: the Section 2 decision trigger ([the Founder sets date], else re-plan), counsel engaged across the four gates, and the Year-0 block, which defines progress no gate can block. Failure here will not look like a decision; it will look like one more productive quarter.

**2. The company pays three salaries before it earns a dollar.** Created by our own decision on 2026-08-10, and the newest entry on this list. From month 1 the company commits **$150,000/year of principal salary** — $12,500/month, against $0 in the bank — funded by an unpapered loan from one member. With no revenue at all, fixed opex of **$14,764/month** — $12,500 of salary, $1,500 of partner expenses, $764 of subscriptions and tooling — plus $9,000 of equipment in month 1 exhausts the $50,000 injection **during month 3**. The Conservative case already breaches the injection *with* its modelled revenue, by $11,155 in month 5. Items 1 and 3 below are what make that a live risk rather than an arithmetic curiosity: the launch gate blocks external revenue and the close motion is unproven, so the two most likely failure modes both end here. Mitigations, in the order they would actually be used: the salary trigger is one cell per principal on the Assumptions sheet and the decision deliberately kept it reversible; a second $50,000 is declared available; and the cheapest fix is the one this list keeps pointing at — sell something. What would make this fatal is treating "no case needs more than the $50,000" as a cash position rather than a modelling result.

**3. The partner admission is mis-structured.** Now a **three-way 50/35/15** with two undefined lanes and an unpapered collaboration adjacent to the IP schedule — permanent consequences waiting on hurried decisions, and the risk grew rather than shrank on 2026-08-10. the Founder holds exactly half and the partners together hold exactly half, so deadlock was not removed by adding a third owner, only re-staffed; the two-party shotgun that used to break it has been deleted with nothing yet in its place. Gate #14 regressed from counsel-ready to hard-blocked. Done right it triples capacity; done wrong it gives away half the company for consideration nobody has written down — one partner's contribution is unrecorded anywhere in the company's files. Mitigations: the tax structure is decided (profits interest, grant-date trap designed out); D10/D11/D12 answered before counsel, not after; no signing with blank lanes; the collaboration papers first; Section 2 discloses all of it.

**4. Warm intros don't close, and the demand thesis was wrong.** The near-term plan rests on relationships converting, and the first signal is mixed: the warmest prospect we will ever have, at founding pricing, sits unsigned at proposal. If warm intros at near-free pricing don't close, cold outbound at full price won't rescue the model. Mitigations: proof-led GTM, the design-partner option, 48-hour speed as the trust weapon, and forcing that first outcome rather than tolerating the silence.

**5. The delivery promise breaks on client #1.** One reliability failure in an early engagement destroys the exact asset we sell. The 48-hour claim is unproven; the first 3–5 deliveries are explicitly white-glove — and **50 clients by end of Year 1** (Target; 25 Conservative) requires the autonomy bet proven by roughly client five, the central unproven bet of the operating model. Mitigations: the eval/approval/watchdog layer is non-negotiable; gates hold over speed; the claim is validated before it is marketed.

**6. We drift into competing on the commodity layer.** If YourCo ever competes on agent tooling rather than reliability, trust, and integration, it has volunteered to fight where it holds no advantage. Mitigations: the moat framing in every planning document; the dividend as the standing sales answer; the parked-SaaS rule.

**7. Founder bandwidth fails.** One person conducts strategy, delivery, and platform, across more than one venture — a named tier-two killer, and the Year-1 plan now asks three principals to carry 50 clients while two of them have not signed anything. The partner admission is partly the remedy and, per item 3, partly a new risk until the lanes are real. Mitigations: AI leverage, hire-when-it-hurts, and the OS itself.

**8. A compliance mistake in a regulated corner.** Outbound email/SMS (CAN-SPAM, TCPA/FTSA), client data, and regulated verticals in the pipeline each carry rules where casual error is expensive. Mitigations: compliance agent + counsel gates, email-first outbound, BAA/DPA for regulated work, and hard stops the gate tracker enforces in writing.

**9. Unit economics invert on heavy clients.** The $300/client/mo absorbed-cost assumption could be badly wrong in either direction — Sample Product models $15–40 for a deterministic engine, while a voice- or volume-heavy OS could exceed $300 and turn the best logos into the worst margins. Either way the 48–50% NOI line is unearned until measured. Mitigations: per-client cost ledgers from day one; the model's Actuals tab, which refuses to compute a measured cost per client until real data exists; pricing authority tied to actual margin data.

What ties these together: none of the top four is a technology risk. The exposure is commercial, structural and now financial — an undefined gate, a salary commitment made before the first sale, an unsigned structure, an unproven close motion. **The ranking changed on 2026-08-10** and the reason is worth stating: the partner admission moved up because adding a third member made it worse rather than better, and a liquidity risk appeared that did not exist that morning because we chose to start paying people. Neither was caused by the market. The technology, ironically, is the part that keeps proving itself.

## 11 · Capital

Bootstrapped, capital-light, AI-leveraged — designed to reach profitability without outside money. The model states the cash position rather than asserting it: **funded by the Founder's $50,000 day-one injection, with a further $50,000 declared available from him**, EBITDA-positive by month 4 (month 4 Aggressive, month 6 Conservative). ⚠ **The first $50,000 does not cover every case.** Once equipment, partner expenses and conferences are charged, the Conservative case runs **$11,155 below zero in month 5** and Target consumes **97%** of the injection — so the second tranche is a requirement of the downside case, not a cushion on top of it. The model still excludes legal, CPA, insurance, payment processing and taxes, so the true hole is larger than $11,155. The injection is a **loan repayable to the Founder**, not permanent capital, and it ranks **behind** member distributions.

**Two further things the cash line does not mean** — both of which survive the Conservative breach above and make it worse, not better. First, **the floor moved on 2026-08-10.** The Conservative column now runs 45% audit→client conversion and 18% annual churn — cell for cell, what was the *Base* case that morning. The workbook states it on its own face: *"the model no longer contains a case below 45% conversion. No prospect has ever been asked to buy — there is still no close rate."* So the three cases are not three tests of the cash position; they are one revenue thesis run at three slopes, with the pessimistic slope removed. Nothing about the business changed to justify that — the floor was raised, exactly as §9 says of the client targets. Second, **no case tests late revenue.** All three sell the first audit in month 2 and bank the first dollar in month 3. Strip the revenue and the arithmetic is short: fixed opex is **$13,264/month** in months 1–2 (three principal draws plus subscriptions and tooling), so with no revenue at all **the injection is exhausted during month 4**. Against a company that has delivered zero audits in 61 days, that — not the scenario spread — is the claim the cash structure actually rests on.

**And neither $50,000 is money the company has.** Cash on hand today is **$0** and runway is 0.0 months. The injection is a modelled opening position at 2026-12-31, unpapered, and a Reserved Matter under an operating agreement nobody has signed. The second $50,000 is one member's declaration — undrawn, in no projection, withdrawable. "Total capital available: $100,000" is true as written and should not be read as a balance sheet.

If capital is ever raised, it buys **GTM acceleration** and **platform engineering** — compressing the Year-3/5 timeline, never funding operations. A raise that funds survival would mean the model failed; a raise that buys speed on a working model is a price-of-time calculation made from strength, if at all.
